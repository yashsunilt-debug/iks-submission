## Games

Ancient Indian games include chaturanga, the ancestor of chess; pachisi and chaupar; dice play; Gyan Chaupar, the original snakes and ladders; pallanguzhi, a seed-sowing game; and contact and chase games such as kabaddi and kho-kho.

A flagstone courtyard with a grid pattern and a small raised stone seat, beside red sandstone pavilions.

The pachisi court at Fatehpur Sikri, a courtyard laid out as a board.

### History

Chaturanga is first named in Banabhatta’s Harshacharita (c. 625 CE) and reached the Islamic world and medieval Europe via Persia. Pachisi courtyards at Agra and Fatehpur Sikri date from the 16th century, and the oldest surviving Gyan Chaupar board discussed is 17th-century, from Mewar. The Mahabharata links claimed for kabaddi and kho-kho are traditional, not documented.

### Ancient textual references

The Rigveda hymn 10.34, the “Gambler’s Lament,” describes a ruined gambler and the dice that destroyed his home. The Mahabharata’s Sabha Parva tells how Yudhishthira loses his kingdom to dice, and wrestling bouts appear in both epics. The earliest usually cited literary mention of mallakhamb is the Manasollasa (c. 1135 CE).

### Rituals and practice

Dice play formed part of the Rajasuya royal consecration. The Jain form of Gyan Chaupar, on 84 squares, was popular during the Paryushan festival. Hindu boards use 72 squares and the Vaishnava Paramapadam uses 100. Pallanguzhi was traditionally played mainly by women and girls in Tamil Nadu and Kerala.

### Cultural meaning

Chaturanga trained strategy and foresight, pachisi staged fate and risk, and Gyan Chaupar taught that winning means reaching liberation rather than beating an opponent. Kabaddi and kho-kho build breath, speed and teamwork with little equipment.

#### 1. Chaturanga (the ancestor of chess)

A carved wooden chess set with figures of camels, elephants and horses.

A carved wooden chess set with camels, elephants and horses, in the spirit of chaturanga.

##### Overview

A two-player strategy board game on an 8×8 board, named for the four divisions of an ancient Indian army: elephants, chariots, cavalry and infantry. Most chess historians treat it as the common ancestor of chess, shogi, xiangqi and related games.

##### History

The clearest early reference comes from Gupta-era north India, around the 6th century CE, and the first known use of the word chaturanga for the game is usually credited to Banabhatta’s Harshacharita (c. 625 CE). From India the game passed to Sassanid Persia as chatrang (later shatranj), then through the Islamic world into medieval Europe. A claim

that chess-like pieces from Lothal (Indus Valley) prove a far older origin is debated and not generally accepted as proof. The exact original rules are unknown; historians reconstruct them from shatranj.

##### Ancient textual references

Harshacharita of Banabhatta (c. 625 CE): earliest known naming of chaturanga.

Bhavishya Purana: sometimes cited as the first description, but this text contains later interpolations (it even mentions British rule), so it is weak evidence for early dating.

The word chaturanga in older Sanskrit meant a four-fold army, and some scholars connect the board name ashtapada (eight-footed, 8×8) to the game’s ancestry. This is interpretation, not proof.

##### Rituals

Limited documentation. Chaturanga is described as a war-simulation and courtly pastime rather than a ritual game.

##### Regional practices

India’s own descendant forms are collectively called Indian chess, and the game’s terminology survives in Persian and Arabic (the bishop is called the “elephant” in Arabic). Regional local variants within India are poorly documented.

##### Cultural meaning

It trained players in military strategy and foresight and reflected the Gupta court’s interest in mathematics and statecraft. A popular legend says a wise man invented it to show a king the value of every subject, however small. This is a story, not history.

#### 2. Pachisi and Chaupar

A pink and black cloth pachisi board with wooden pieces and two dice sticks.

A pachisi cloth board with wooden pieces and dice sticks.

##### Overview

A cross-and-circle race game for two to four players. Pachisi uses cowrie shells as dice; Chaupar (Chausar) uses oblong dice or sticks. The name comes from Hindi pachis (twenty-five), the highest throw.

##### History

Often called India’s national table game. Although traditionally said to be ancient, the oldest firm evidence is physical: the large Mughal-era pachisi courtyards at Agra and Fatehpur Sikri (16th century). Akbar’s court historian Abu’l Fazl records that the people of Hindustan had long loved the game. Emperor Akbar is reported to have played it with living pieces on a giant flagstone board. Penn Museum scholarship notes that the equipment (oblong dice, cowries, cruciform board) is far older than the 16th century, even if the game as a whole cannot be dated earlier. The British later adapted it as Ludo (patented 1896) and, in the US, “Sorry!”.

##### Ancient textual references

Tradition links the dice game of the Mahabharata to chaupar, and some modern sources say Pachisi is mentioned there as “Pasha”. Specialist scholars caution that the epic’s descriptions do not clearly match pachisi. Treat the Mahabharata link as plausible tradition. See also Section 3.

##### Rituals

Limited documentation for pachisi itself. Its related dice-gambling in royal courts is connected to ritual dice play in the Rajasuya sacrifice (Section 3).

##### Regional practices

A Tamil variant called pagade is still played. Boards survive on cloth and paper in museums including the National Museum, New Delhi, the British Museum and the V&A. Some sources say the royals played chaupar with wooden dice while pachisi with cowries was the commoners’ version; this is a popular claim, not firmly documented.

##### Cultural meaning

A game of fate and skill that symbolises the royal court’s leisure, wealth and risk. The Mahabharata association gives it a moral weight: the danger of staking everything on chance.

#### 3. Dyuta: Vedic and epic dice gaming

##### Overview

Dyuta means gambling or dice play. It is the best-documented ancient Indian game in the texts, from the Rigveda onwards.

##### History

Dice (akṣa) were made from the nuts of the vibhītaka tree (Terminalia bellirica). The scoring faces were named after the four ages: kṛ ta (four), tretā (three), dvāpara (two) and kali (one). In the Rigveda the dice are an obsession that ruins a household. In the epics, dice gambling brings down kings.

##### Ancient textual references

Rigveda 10.34, the “Gambler’s Lament” (Akṣasūkta): a 14-verse hymn in triṣṭubh metre in which a ruined gambler speaks of the dice that destroyed his home, and ends with a verse in which the god Savitṛ tells him to stop gambling and farm his own field. Scholars regard it as one of the few Rigvedic hymns without a direct ritual function.

Mahabharata, Sabha Parva: Yudhishthira loses his kingdom, brothers, himself and Draupadi to Shakuni’s dice, a root cause of the Kurukshetra war.

Nala and Damayanti (Mahabharata, Vana Parva): Nala loses his kingdom at dice.

Dharmashastras: these regulate gambling (public venue, supervisor, vibhitaka dice); a source I used says the Manusmriti (IX.220–28) advises kings against it. I verified this only through a secondary source, so check the verse numbers in a critical edition.

##### Rituals

Hindupedia notes that an arranged dice-play forms a ritual part of the Rajasuya royal consecration. Dice gaming therefore had a ritual-political face as well as a leisure one.

##### Regional practices

Limited documentation in the sources I found. Later folk practices (e.g. festival-season gambling customs) were not verified, so I have left them out.

##### Cultural meaning

The Rigveda hymn is both psychology and warning: the dice personified as an irresistible, honey-coated force. The epic version turns the same idea into a tragedy about duty (dharma), kingship and the cost of addiction.

#### 4. Gyan Chaupar / Moksha Patam / Paramapadam (the original

Snakes and Ladders)

A painted cloth game board with ladders, snakes and a temple at the top.

A painted cloth Gyan Chaupar, with ladders for virtues and snakes for vices.

##### Overview

A dice-and-board game in which ladders stand for virtues that lift a soul and snakes for vices that pull it down, with the final square symbolising moksha (liberation). Also called Parampadam, Vaikunthapali, Jnana Bazi.

##### History

Played in medieval India and Nepal as both recreation and moral teaching. The oldest surviving example discussed by the Impart Encyclopedia is a 17th-century board from Mewar. It reached Britain in the 1890s as “Snakes and Ladders”, stripped of its religious content and with snakes and ladders evened out, and arrived in the US in 1943 as Chutes and Ladders.

##### Ancient textual references

No single ancient text describes it. Its concepts come from Jain and Hindu theology (karma, maya, dharma, moksha). Some writers claim much older origins, but the dated evidence I found is medieval. Treat claims of a Vedic or very ancient origin as unproven.

##### Rituals

The Jain version was especially popular during the Paryushan festival, when devotees fasted and played it as spiritual engagement. Boards were laid on cloth or paper, often with decorative imagery.

##### Regional practices

Hindu boards: most commonly 72 squares.

Jain boards (Gujarat and Rajasthan): commonly 84 squares, linked to Jain cosmology.

Vaishnava/Tamil form (Paramapadam): a 100-square layout leading to Vaikuntha, the abode of Vishnu.

A rare Sufi board is also reported in Indian sources.

##### Cultural meaning

A teaching tool for ethics. The dice stand for fate, the moves for choices. Winning is reaching liberation, not beating an opponent.

#### 5. Pallanguzhi (Mancala of South India)

A carved wooden board with rows of round pits and cowrie shells.

A wooden pallanguzhi board with cowrie shells.

##### Overview

A two-player seed-sowing (mancala) game played on a board with two rows of seven pits, using tamarind seeds, cowrie shells or small stones. Names include Pallanguzhi/Pallankuli (Tamil, Malayalam), Alaguli Mane (Kannada), Vamana Guntalu (Telugu).

##### History

One study reports that the term appears in Pallava copper plates (about 550 CE) and that the game is carved in the Shore Temple, Mamallapuram (about 700 CE). That paper is a secondary source, so confirm the inscriptions with epigraphic references. Some enthusiasts claim rock-cut pits in Tamil Nadu are 10,000 years old; this is a contested claim, not an accepted dating. Whether the mancala family spread from Africa, Arabia or India remains unsettled. Note that the claim in one blog that the Arabic word mancala is derived from Tamil is not accepted scholarship.

##### Ancient textual references

Limited documentation of the game itself in classical texts. The evidence is epigraphic and archaeological (copper plates, temple carvings) as reported above.

##### Rituals

No formal ritual. The game is closely tied to the idea of sowing and harvest.

##### Regional practices

Traditionally played mainly by women and girls in homes, on wooden boards or on the ground, in Tamil Nadu and Kerala, later also in Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh. Related games are found in Sri Lanka, Malaysia, Indonesia (congkak) and the Philippines (sungka). Enthusiast groups in Chennai and elsewhere are reviving it as a maths-learning game.

##### Cultural meaning

A game of quick counting and planning that trained children in arithmetic, and a femalecentred domestic space for sociability.

#### 6. Aadu Puli Attam (Goats and Tigers)

A Tiger and Goats board with a triangular layout of lines and pieces.

An Aadu Puli Attam (Tiger and Goats) board, with its Tamil title.

##### Overview

A two-player hunt game in which one side plays tigers and the other goats on a triangular or lined board. Known as Adu Huli (Karnataka) and Puli Meka (Andhra Pradesh).

##### History

Often said to be over a thousand years old, but I found no dated source for that figure. The strongest evidence is physical: boards carved into temple floors across Tamil Nadu, in many variants with different numbers of tigers and goats.

##### Ancient textual references

Limited documentation. Historian V. Balambal discusses Tamil board games, including Aadu Puli Attam, as variations on the chaturanga tradition of strategy games; that classification is hers and is not universally accepted.

##### Rituals

No formal ritual found. The temple setting suggests informal play by pilgrims and temple visitors.

##### Regional practices

South India, especially Tamil Nadu. Carvings are reported at temples including Madurai Meenakshi Amman, according to Tamil Nadu press reporting. Local researchers say that the history of Tamil games rests largely on oral tradition and carved evidence rather than written records.

##### Cultural meaning

A predator-and-prey game, often used today in teaching about ecosystems and strategy.

#### 7. Kabaddi

A raider in the air above defenders, in an older photograph.

A kabaddi raid, in an older photograph.

##### Overview

A team contact sport: a raider enters the opposing half chanting “kabaddi” in a single breath, tags defenders and returns. The name is often linked to the Tamil kai-pidi (holding hands), a popular but unproven derivation.

##### History

Tradition places its origin in Tamil Nadu and links it to the Mahabharata and to Buddhist literature (the Buddha is said to have played it for recreation). It was standardised as a competitive sport in the 20th century, featured in the 1936 Berlin Olympics as a demonstration, entered the Asian Games in 1990, and became widely watched through the Pro Kabaddi League.

##### Ancient textual references

The usual citation is Abhimanyu’s fight inside the chakravyuha in the Mahabharata, which some writers say resembles raid-and-defend tactics. This is an analogy drawn by later writers, not a description of a game in the epic. Sangam-era Tamil literature is also cited for related contest games; verify specific poems before citing them.

##### Rituals

Limited documentation. Folk forms are associated with village festivals and youth gatherings.

##### Regional practices

Played across India under regional names, with strongest traditions in Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra, Punjab and Haryana. Variants include circle-style and standard (Punjabi) forms. The sport is also widely played in Bangladesh, Iran and South Korea.

##### Cultural meaning

A sport of breath, nerve and teamwork, requiring no equipment. It became a symbol of rural India’s sporting identity.

#### 8. Kho-Kho

Players in orange and blue at a kho-kho match, with chasers running past crouched players.

A kho-kho match, with chasers running past players crouched in a line.

##### Overview

A tag game of two teams: nine chasers (seven on court, with the others in reserve) pursue defenders around a line of sitting players, with the “kho” call passing the chase to a seated player.

##### History

Usually described as one of the oldest outdoor tag sports in India. Wikipedia cites play since at least the 4th century BCE, and some regional writers say it began in Maharashtra as a chariot-based game (ratheras); neither claim is backed by a primary text I could verify. It was demonstrated at the 1936 Berlin Olympics. The Kho Kho Federation of India was founded in 1955, launched Ultimate Kho Kho in 2022 and, with the International Kho Kho Federation, hosted the first Kho Kho World Cup in January 2025.

##### Ancient textual references

As with kabaddi, writers link it to the chakravyuha episode of the Mahabharata. This is interpretive, not a textual description of the game.

##### Rituals

Limited documentation.

##### Regional practices

Strongest in Maharashtra and widely played in schools across India. Exported with Indian migrants to the Caribbean, Africa and other parts of Asia. Now played in about 26 member countries of the International Kho Kho Federation.

##### Cultural meaning

Speed, agility and teamwork with minimal equipment. Its inclusion in school sport and Khelo India programmes reflects a revival of indigenous sport.

#### 9. Mallakhamb

##### Overview

A gymnastic discipline performed on a vertical wooden pole (or hanging rope), combining yoga-like postures, wrestling holds and strength. From Marathi malla (wrestler) and khamb (pole).

##### History

Originated in Maharashtra as a training method for wrestlers. The earliest direct literary mention usually cited is the Manasollasa (also called Abhilashitartha Chintamani), a Sanskrit encyclopedia attributed to the Chalukya king Someshvara III (c. 1135 CE). It was revived in the early 19th century and is reported to have been practised by figures of the 1857 uprising, including Rani Lakshmibai (secondary-source claim). A team demonstrated it at the 1936 Berlin Olympics.

##### Ancient textual references

Manasollasa (c. 1135 CE), as above. Earlier ancient references are not established.

##### Rituals

Limited documentation in the sources I found. Pole-climbing is commonly associated with strength-training in wrestling akharas and with devotion to Hanuman \[verify; general knowledge, not from the linked sources\].

##### Regional practices

Maharashtra (strongest), Madhya Pradesh and Gujarat. Variants: pole, hanging and rope mallakhamb. The pole is traditionally made of sheesham (Indian rosewood) and polished with castor oil.

##### Cultural meaning

It fuses discipline, control and aesthetics, and is identified under the Khelo India scheme among the indigenous sports for promotion.

#### 10. Malla-yuddha and Kushti (wrestling)

Two wrestlers in a sand pit in front of a red building.

Wrestlers in a sand pit.

##### Overview

Hand-to-hand combat sport, called malla-yuddha in Sanskrit sources and later kushti, pehlwani or dangal in northern India.

##### History

Dates of five centuries BCE are often given for Indian wrestling. The modern kushti is described as a blend of Indian malla-yuddha with Persian koshti influence from the Mughal period. Training is traditionally done in the akhara, an earthen gym.

##### Ancient textual references

Both the Ramayana and the Mahabharata describe wrestling bouts, including those of Bhima and Jarasandha in the Mahabharata (Sabha Parva). Verse-level citations were not verified for this document.

##### Rituals

Akhara life traditionally involves discipline, diet and devotion, often to Hanuman \[verify; general knowledge, not from the linked sources\].

##### Regional practices

Spread across north India (Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh) and Maharashtra (Kolhapur is well known as a centre).

##### Cultural meaning

A path of self-discipline and community identity, still tied to village fairs and festivals.

## Dance

Dance in India spans the classical forms commonly listed by the Sangeet Natak Akademi, Chhau, devotional dance-drama and folk traditions. The Nāṭyaśāstra gives them a shared vocabulary of abhinaya, rasa, bhava and mudra, but each living tradition has its own history.

A bronze statue of Shiva dancing inside a ring of flames.

A bronze Nataraja, Shiva as the lord of dance.

### History

Bharatanatyam took its modern stage form in the twentieth century from Tamil temple and court traditions. Kathak grew from kathakaras, or storytellers, and was refined under Mughal and Awadh patronage. Kathakali took shape in Kerala in the seventeenth century, Odissi was systematised in the twentieth, and Sattriya grew in Assamese monasteries from the reforms of Srimanta Sankardeva. Ancient treatises describe a performance vocabulary, not these forms by name.

### Ancient textual references

The Nāṭyaśāstra, attributed to Bharata and dated broadly to the last centuries BCE and early centuries CE, is the main theoretical source. Nandikeśvara’s Abhinaya Darpaṇa, the Abhinaya Chandrikā for Odissi and the Bhagavata Purana add technique and narrative. Temple sculpture at Chidambaram and Konark shows poses but does not prove that present repertoires existed unchanged.

### Rituals and performance

Odissi is connected with temple dancers (Mahari) and the Gotipua tradition of young boys. Sattriya belongs to monastic worship, Chhau is performed at the spring festival Chaitra Parva, and Kathakali’s elaborate make-up is a preparatory practice in itself. Folk forms follow the farming year and life events, such as Bihu in Assam, Garba and Dandiya Raas in Gujarat, and Bhangra and Giddha in Punjab.

### Cultural meaning

The research treats dance as an integrated knowledge system: embodied movement, aesthetic rasa, treatises, seasonal calendars, social roles, and the craft of costume and instruments. It notes that supportive practice can aid well-being, while excessive pressure, criticism and overtraining can cause stress.

#### 1. Introduction, Scope and Ancient Foundations

Dance in India is not a single art form but a wide family of performance traditions. It includes codified classical systems, devotional dance-drama, seasonal and community dances, ritual performances and martial or theatrical forms. Across regions, dance has communicated stories, worship, social identity, seasonal knowledge, emotion and collective memory.

A bronze statue of Shiva dancing inside a ring of flames.

A bronze Nataraja, Shiva as the lord of dance.

##### Scope and method

This study gives detailed profiles of the eight classical forms commonly listed by the Sangeet Natak Akademi—Bharatanatyam, Kathak, Kathakali, Kuchipudi, Manipuri, Mohiniyattam, Odissi and Sattriya—followed by Chhau and selected folk traditions. Each profile uses the same headings: history, textual references, rituals and performance setting, regional practice, and cultural meaning. Government and cultural-institution sources, UNESCO records, academic publications and classical-text resources are prioritised.

##### The Nāṭyaśāstra: a shared theoretical foundation

The Sanskrit Nāṭyaśāstra, traditionally attributed to Bharata, is a foundational treatise on dramaturgy and performance. Modern scholarship commonly places its formation across a broad period around the last centuries BCE and early centuries CE; the exact date and layers of composition remain debated. It discusses theatre, acting, music, stagecraft, movement and audience response. It is therefore a major source for understanding the intellectual world of Indian performance, but it should not be treated as a dated birth certificate for every regional dance tradition.

##### Key concepts

Abhinaya is expressive communication through body, speech, costume and inner emotional states. Rasa refers to the aesthetic experience evoked in the audience; bhāva refers to the emotional states represented. Mudrā denotes a codified hand gesture. Later teaching often distinguishes nṛtta (abstract or pure movement), nṛtya (expressive dance) and nāṭya (dramatic representation). These categories help explain shared principles, although each living tradition interprets them in its own way.

##### Evidence note

A text may describe a broad performance vocabulary without describing a modern dance style by its present name. For this reason, the study separates (1) direct textual references, (2) later commentarial or regional traditions, and (3) modern reconstructions and institutional descriptions. Claims of uninterrupted survival from a remote ancient period should be treated carefully unless supported by dated evidence.

#### 2. Bharatanatyam — Tamil Nadu

A dancer in a maroon and gold costume, in a raised pose.

A Bharatanatyam dancer in a pleated costume with gold jewellery.

##### Region and identity

This profile examines the dance tradition associated with Tamil Nadu, placing its history within the wider diversity of Indian performance.

##### History

Bharatanatyam is associated especially with Tamil Nadu and with temple, court and hereditary performance communities. Its modern stage form developed through major twentieth-century changes in patronage, pedagogy and public presentation.

The older repertoire is connected with the devadāsī and nattuvanar traditions of temples and courts; colonial-era social reform and the decline of older patronage altered these institutions. Artists and institutions later reorganised the form for the modern proscenium stage. It is inaccurate to reduce this complex history to a simple claim that the present form has remained unchanged since antiquity.

##### Ancient textual references

The Nāṭyaśāstra provides a broad grammar of gesture, posture, rhythm and dramatic expression relevant to many classical styles. The Tamil region also preserves a long history of dance imagery in temple sculpture, including the famous 108 karaṇa panels at Chidambaram. Later treatises such as Nandikeśvara’s Abhinaya Darpaṇa are important in teaching gesture and expression. These sources illuminate technique and ideals; they do not document Bharatanatyam under its current name in the ancient period.

##### Rituals and performance setting

Older temple contexts included devotional service, dance offerings and performance on festival occasions. Present-day Bharatanatyam commonly opens with an invocation and may include an alarippu, jatisvaram, śabdam, varṇam, padam and tillāna, though repertoire varies. Abhinaya may interpret devotional poetry addressed to Shiva, Vishnu, Krishna or the Goddess. The stage performance is now also a concert art, educational discipline and part of cultural festivals.

##### Regional practices

Strongly rooted in Tamil Nadu, especially Chennai and historic temple centres, Bharatanatyam is now taught and performed throughout India and internationally. Distinct bani or stylistic lineages are associated with teachers and institutions, with differences in movement quality, repertoire, rhythm and expressive approach. Costume commonly uses a pleated silk dance sari, temple jewellery, ankle bells and expressive eye and hand work.

##### Cultural meaning

The form brings together devotion, disciplined bodily training, poetry, music and aesthetic storytelling. Its repertoire preserves Tamil and Sanskrit devotional literature while also accommodating contemporary themes. It has become a major symbol of Tamil cultural identity and a globally recognised Indian performance art.

#### 3. Kathak — North India

A dancer in a gold costume kneeling with one arm raised.

A dancer in a gold costume, in a kneeling pose.

##### Region and identity

This profile examines the dance tradition associated with North India, placing its history within the wider diversity of Indian performance.

##### History

The word Kathak is associated with kathā, meaning story or narration, and kathākāras—storytellers who communicated episodes from epics and devotional traditions. Over time, the form developed in temple, public and royal-court settings. During the Mughal and later Awadh periods, court patronage shaped its refined technique, repertoire and musical presentation. Lucknow, Jaipur and Banaras are commonly discussed gharānā lineages, each with distinctive emphases.

Modern Kathak is a concert and teaching tradition across India.

##### Ancient textual references

Kathak draws on the wider Sanskrit performance vocabulary found in the Nāṭyaśāstra and later works on gesture and dramaturgy. However, the historical development of Kathak as a named north Indian style is better documented in medieval and early modern performance histories than in a single ancient text. Stories from the Rāmāyaṇa, Mahābhārata, Purāṇas and Krishna-bhakti literature form an important part of its narrative repertoire.

##### Rituals and performance setting

Temple narration and devotional storytelling are part of its historical background, while court performances encouraged virtuoso rhythm, improvisation and refinement. A modern recital may include vandanā, thāṭ, amad, tukṛā, toṛā, paran, tihāī and gat-bhāva. Footwork is articulated through ankle bells, and performers interact with tabla and melodic accompaniment. These are performance conventions rather than obligatory religious rites.

##### Regional practices

Kathak is associated particularly with Uttar Pradesh and north India, with major centres in Lucknow, Jaipur, Varanasi and Delhi. The Lucknow gharānā is often noted for expressive abhinaya and grace; Jaipur for complex rhythmic work and energetic footwork; Banaras for its own repertoire and stylistic features. These descriptions are broad tendencies, not rigid rules.

##### Cultural meaning

Kathak demonstrates cultural exchange: storytelling, bhakti, Persianate court culture, Hindustani music and modern theatre all contributed to its evolution. It communicates narrative through rhythm, gesture, gaze, turns and improvisation, showing how performance traditions adapt while retaining recognisable lineages.

#### 4. Kathakali — Kerala

A Kathakali performer with green face make-up and a red and gold headdress.

A Kathakali performer in green face make-up and a large headdress.

##### Region and identity

This profile examines the dance tradition associated with Kerala, placing its history within the wider diversity of Indian performance.

##### History

Kathakali is a highly stylised dance-drama of Kerala that took shape in the seventeenth century through earlier temple and performance traditions, including Krishnanattam and Ramanattam. It combines dance, acting, music, elaborate costume, facial make-up and percussion. Its development was supported by local rulers and performance communities. Training traditionally required years of disciplined practice, including body conditioning and control of facial expression.

##### Ancient textual references

The Nāṭyaśāstra is an important theoretical reference for dramatic representation, rasa, bhāva and movement, but Kathakali itself is a later regional form. Its stories are often drawn from the Mahābhārata, Rāmāyaṇa and Purāṇas, adapted through Malayalam and Sanskrit literary traditions. A key historical source for the form’s development is the early modern tradition of Ramanattam, associated with the Kottarakkara court.

##### Rituals and performance setting

Traditional performances were often held in temple courtyards and open-air venues, sometimes continuing through the night. The make-up process is itself an elaborate preparatory practice. Character types are signalled by colour and facial design: green-faced noble heroes, red or black features for other character types, and special white facial framing for certain roles. The performance uses hand gestures, eye movements, facial muscles and stylised body action to narrate episodes.

##### Regional practices

Kathakali belongs to Kerala and is sustained by training centres, temple-linked performance networks, cultural institutions and festivals. The orchestra commonly includes chenda, maddalam, cymbals and vocalists. Costumes use layered skirts, large headgear and vivid face designs. The form has also developed shorter stage presentations for contemporary audiences.

##### Cultural meaning

Kathakali is a dramatic visual language through which communities encounter epic narratives, ethical conflicts and devotional themes. Its elaborate make-up and codified character types turn the performer into a symbolic figure. It is also an important example of Kerala’s integrated arts, combining dance, theatre, music, painting and craft.

#### 5. Kuchipudi — Andhra Pradesh

##### Region and identity

This profile examines the dance tradition associated with Andhra Pradesh, placing its history within the wider diversity of Indian performance.

##### History

Kuchipudi takes its name from a village in present-day Andhra Pradesh. It developed as a dance-drama tradition associated with Bhagavata Mela and Vaishnava storytelling. Siddhendra Yogi is traditionally credited with shaping an important part of its repertoire, especially the dance-drama Bhāmākalāpam. Historically, male troupes performed many roles, including female characters; women became increasingly prominent in the modern concert form.

##### Ancient textual references

The Nāṭyaśāstra and Abhinaya Darpaṇa offer concepts of movement, gesture and dramatic expression that are relevant to Kuchipudi practice. The tradition’s narrative material draws on the Bhāgavata Purāṇa and other devotional literature, particularly stories of Krishna. These textual connections explain the repertoire, but the specific Kuchipudi style is a regional historical development rather than a dance explicitly named in the ancient treatises.

##### Rituals and performance setting

Traditional Kuchipudi was often performed as a dance-drama in temple and village festival contexts. The performance could include invocatory pieces, character introductions, expressive episodes and ensemble scenes. A well-known stage feature is the tarangam, in which a dancer may perform on the rim of a brass plate while balancing a pot, demonstrating rhythmic control. It is a celebrated stage convention, not a requirement in every performance.

##### Regional practices

The form is rooted in the Kuchipudi village of Andhra Pradesh and is now taught in academies and universities across India. It combines brisk footwork, rounded movement, expressive storytelling and Carnatic music. Costumes resemble the pleated silk attire of other southern classical styles, adapted to Kuchipudi’s movement vocabulary.

##### Cultural meaning

Kuchipudi joins devotional storytelling with theatre and virtuoso dance. It preserves Telugu literary and musical traditions and demonstrates the role of village communities, teachers and performance lineages in sustaining a classical art. Its evolution from group dance-drama to solo and ensemble stage presentations reflects changing audiences and institutions.

#### 6. Odissi — Odisha

Two dancers in red and cream costumes in front of stone carvings.

Two dancers in red and cream costumes in front of temple carvings.

##### Region and identity

This profile examines the dance tradition associated with Odisha, placing its history within the wider diversity of Indian performance.

##### History

Odissi is associated with Odisha’s temple and performance cultures. Its history is connected with the Mahari tradition of women who performed in temple contexts and the Gotipua tradition, in which young boys performed devotional dance.

Sculptural evidence from Odisha’s temples, including Konark, provides important visual documentation of dance poses and performance culture. The modern concert form was reconstructed and systematised in the twentieth century by gurus, performers and institutions.

##### Ancient textual references

The Nāṭyaśāstra supplies a broader vocabulary of movement and expression, while the regional treatise Abhinaya Chandrikā is particularly important in discussions of Odissi technique. Temple sculptures, inscriptions and regional devotional literature add historical evidence. The term Odissi as a modern classical category reflects a later process of codification; the presence of dance sculptures alone does not prove that the present repertoire existed unchanged in antiquity.

##### Rituals and performance setting

Mahari performances were historically connected with temple service and ritual calendars. Contemporary Odissi recitals may begin with maṅgalācaraṇa, followed by items such as batu, pallavi, abhinaya and mokṣa, with variations among gurus and schools. Devotional compositions often address Jagannath, Krishna, Shiva and the Goddess. The tribhaṅga body posture—three bends of the body—is a signature visual feature.

##### Regional practices

Odissi is rooted in Odisha, particularly the cultural landscape of Puri, Bhubaneswar and Konark. Its music, poetry, costume and jewellery draw on Odia traditions. The Gotipua tradition remains an important related practice and a source for understanding the movement vocabulary and transmission of the art.

##### Cultural meaning

Odissi connects dance with Jagannath culture, temple sculpture, Odia poetry and regional identity. It translates sculptural stillness into movement and uses lyric expression to communicate devotion and emotion. Its revival also demonstrates how communities and scholars reinterpret heritage in changing social circumstances.

#### 7. Manipuri — Manipur

A dancer in a red costume standing in front of a stone temple.

A dancer in a red costume in front of a stone temple.

##### Region and identity

This profile examines the dance tradition associated with Manipur, placing its history within the wider diversity of Indian performance.

##### History

Manipuri dance developed in the cultural and religious environment of Manipur. The region has older indigenous ritual and dance practices, while Vaishnavism—especially devotion to Krishna—strongly shaped the repertoire from the eighteenth century onward. Ras Lila became a central devotional dance-drama, supported by royal patronage and community participation. Manipuri also includes forms such as Lai Haraoba, which preserve ritual narratives and local cosmologies.

##### Ancient textual references

Manipuri’s textual background is layered. The Bhagavata Purāṇa and Vaishnava devotional literature inform Ras Lila themes, while local oral traditions and ritual knowledge are essential to Lai Haraoba. The Nāṭyaśāstra is a comparative theoretical reference, but the dance’s characteristic movement and devotional repertory developed within Manipur’s own history and should not be reduced to a direct derivation from the Sanskrit treatise.

##### Rituals and performance setting

Ras Lila is performed in devotional settings and during religious festivals, with episodes centred on Krishna, Radha and the gopis. Lai Haraoba is a ritual festival performance honouring local deities and recounting creation-related narratives through song, dance and symbolic movement. Manipuri dance is often recognised for rounded, flowing movement and restrained facial expression. The pung (drum) and cymbals are important in associated music traditions.

##### Regional practices

Manipur is the heartland of the form. Ras Lila, Pung Cholom (vigorous drum dance), Kartal Cholom and Lai Haraoba demonstrate the range of the region’s performance culture. Women’s Ras costumes often include the distinctive cylindrical skirt, while male performers wear dhoti-based attire. The Jawaharlal Nehru Manipur Dance Academy has played a major role in training and preservation.

##### Cultural meaning

Manipuri dance expresses devotion, community memory and the relationship between local traditions and Vaishnava bhakti. It also demonstrates that regional ritual forms can coexist with and reshape pan-Indian religious narratives. The tradition remains a strong marker of Manipuri cultural identity.

#### 8. Mohiniyattam — Kerala

A dancer in a white costume with orange and gold borders.

A dancer in a white costume with gold borders.

##### Region and identity

This profile examines the dance tradition associated with Kerala, placing its history within the wider diversity of Indian performance.

##### History

Mohiniyattam is a solo dance tradition of Kerala, historically associated with feminine grace and the lasya mode. Its name is commonly interpreted through Mohini, the enchanting form of Vishnu. The form developed in Kerala’s temple and courtly cultural environment and was shaped by regional music, costume and movement traditions. It underwent renewed institutional development in the twentieth century, including work associated with Kerala Kalamandalam.

##### Ancient textual references

The Nāṭyaśāstra’s discussions of lasya, abhinaya and movement provide a broad theoretical context. Mohiniyattam’s name and devotional themes draw on Hindu mythological traditions, particularly stories of Vishnu as Mohini. Yet the specific form is historically regional; it is not accurately described as a complete dance system directly set out in the ancient treatise.

##### Rituals and performance setting

Traditional performance contexts included temple and courtly settings, while modern Mohiniyattam is usually presented as a solo recital. Repertoire may include chollukettu, jatisvaram, varṇam, padam and tillāna. The dance often uses gentle swaying, circular movement and expressive storytelling. Performers commonly wear an off-white or cream costume with gold borders, with hair arranged to one side and traditional jewellery.

##### Regional practices

Kerala is the home region. The form is accompanied by Carnatic-influenced music and Kerala percussion, with Malayalam and Sanskrit compositions. Training institutions and state cultural bodies have supported its teaching, documentation and stage presentation. The style’s soft movement quality is often contrasted with the forceful dramatic vocabulary of Kathakali, though both are diverse traditions.

##### Cultural meaning

Mohiniyattam expresses beauty, devotion and emotional nuance through measured movement and lyrical music. It represents Kerala’s contribution to India’s classical dance landscape and shows how a regional aesthetic can develop into a widely taught national art form.

#### 9. Sattriya — Assam

Two performers on a dark stage, in white and red costumes.

Two performers on stage in white and red costumes.

##### Region and identity

This profile examines the dance tradition associated with Assam, placing its history within the wider diversity of Indian performance.

##### History

Sattriya emerged in Assam’s Vaishnavite monasteries, known as sattras, as part of the devotional movement associated with Srimanta Sankardeva (1449–1568) and his disciple Madhavdeva. It developed within the ankiya nāṭ and bhāona performance traditions, combining dance, music, drama and religious teaching. For centuries, it was preserved within monastic institutions; it later entered wider public stages and received recognition as a classical dance form in 2000.

##### Ancient textual references

Sattriya is not an ancient Vedic dance system. Its strongest textual and historical context lies in the sixteenth-century Assamese Vaishnavite works and performance culture of Sankardeva and Madhavdeva. The Bhāgavata Purāṇa is a major source for devotional narratives, while the Nāṭyaśāstra offers a wider theoretical framework for movement and dramatic performance.

##### Rituals and performance setting

Within the sattra, dance is connected with worship, prayer and the presentation of devotional drama. Performances may be part of religious observances and community gatherings. Major components include mati akhara training exercises and choreographic items such as gayan-bayan, which combines singing and drumming, and dance sequences using khol and tāl. Costume and repertoire vary by tradition and by the role performed.

##### Regional practices

Assam is the principal region, with Majuli and other sattra centres important to its history. The form is performed by both male and female dancers today. Traditional costumes use Assamese textiles such as pat and muga silk, and musical accompaniment includes khol drums, cymbals and devotional singing.

##### Cultural meaning

Sattriya embodies the Assamese neo-Vaishnavite movement and its emphasis on devotion, collective worship, ethical teaching and artistic discipline. It preserves regional language, music, textile traditions and monastic cultural history. Its transition from monastery-centred practice to public stage demonstrates continuity alongside change.

#### 10. Chhau — Eastern India

Chhau is a powerful dance tradition of eastern India, associated with Seraikella (Jharkhand), Purulia (West Bengal) and Mayurbhanj (Odisha). Its vocabulary combines martial movement, stylised animal and bird movements, dramatic gesture and narrative episodes. UNESCO inscribed Chhau on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2010.

##### History

Chhau developed through regional performance, martial practices and community festivals. The three styles have distinct histories and visual vocabularies. Seraikella and Purulia Chhau use masks, while Mayurbhanj Chhau is performed without masks. The form has been sustained by traditional artist families, local communities and festival networks.

##### Ancient textual references

Chhau draws on stories from the Rāmāyaṇa and Mahābhārata, local folklore and abstract themes. These epics provide narrative material, but no ancient Sanskrit text has been established as a direct manual for the modern three Chhau styles. Its strongest documentation is regional and performance-based.

##### Rituals and performance setting

Chhau is closely connected with the spring festival Chaitra Parva and is traditionally performed in open spaces, often at night. The music features reed instruments such as mohuri or shehnai and powerful drums. Masks, where used, are crafted with distinctive facial expressions and ornamentation. The festival setting connects performance with seasonal renewal and community gathering.

##### Regional practices

Seraikella Chhau is known for refined masked movement; Purulia Chhau for large, colourful masks and vigorous leaps;

Mayurbhanj Chhau for athletic movement without masks. The styles share a regional family resemblance but should not be treated as identical. Training is often transmitted through local gurus and artist communities.

##### Cultural meaning

Chhau joins physical discipline, storytelling, craft, music and collective celebration. Its performances bring epic characters and local ideas into a community space. UNESCO notes that the tradition helps bind people across social and linguistic differences, while also identifying pressures on transmission and participation.

#### 12. Ritual, Community and Performance: How Dance Lives

Indian dance traditions are best understood through the settings in which people learn, perform, watch and remember them. The same movement can carry different meanings in a temple, monastery, village festival, royal court, public theatre or contemporary auditorium.

#### 1. Temple and devotional settings

Bharatanatyam, Odissi, Kuchipudi and several regional forms have histories connected with temple service, devotional poetry or festival performance. Sattriya grew in Vaishnavite monasteries, while Manipuri Ras Lila is a devotional enactment of Krishna stories. The exact role of ritual varies by form and period; modern stage performances are not automatically religious ceremonies.

#### 2. Seasonal and agrarian festivals

Bihu accompanies important points in Assam’s agricultural calendar. Garba and Dandiya are widely associated with Navratri in Gujarat. Bhangra is linked with Punjab’s harvest celebrations, while many regional dances accompany sowing, harvest, monsoon, weddings and fairs. Dance can therefore encode local knowledge of seasons, crops and community relations.

#### 3. Life-cycle and social occasions

Wedding celebrations, births, coming-of-age events and community gatherings create occasions for participatory dance. Ghoomar, Giddha and many local forms are social practices as much as performances. Their meaning comes from participation, shared song, humour, memory and the transmission of movement between generations.

#### 4. Music, costume and craft

Dance depends on a network of allied arts: weaving, embroidery, jewellery-making, mask-making, make-up, percussion, singing and instrument-making. Kathakali’s elaborate face designs, Chhau masks, Assamese silk textiles, Odissi silver ornaments and regional dance costumes are not merely decoration; they help communicate character, place and performance identity.

#### 5. Transmission and change

Knowledge passes through gurus, families, monasteries, community groups, schools and cultural institutions. Modernisation has created new stages, audiences and careers, while also changing older patronage systems. Documentation, fair opportunities for practitioners, local-language teaching and support for traditional artists are important to safeguarding living heritage.

##### A useful IKS interpretation

Dance can be studied as an integrated knowledge system: embodied knowledge (movement and balance), aesthetic knowledge (rasa and expression), textual knowledge (treatises and poetry), ecological knowledge (seasonal calendars), social knowledge (community roles), and material knowledge (costume, instruments and craft). This approach avoids treating dance as entertainment alone.

##### Conclusion

The dance traditions of India reveal a continuous conversation between text and practice, devotion and entertainment, individual artistry and community participation. The Nāṭyaśāstra offers a significant theoretical foundation for thinking about movement, expression and audience experience, but regional traditions have their own histories, institutions and vocabularies. Bharatanatyam, Kathak, Kathakali, Kuchipudi, Odissi, Manipuri, Mohiniyattam and Sattriya demonstrate the range of classical practice; Chhau and folk traditions show the vitality of community performance. Together they form a diverse, living heritage rather than a single uniform tradition.

Some dance histories are preserved more strongly through oral transmission, community practice, temple sculpture or later performance manuals than through ancient written records. Where an exact origin date or direct textual reference is uncertain, this paper states the limitation rather than presenting a legend as established fact. #### 16. Findings, Original Understanding and Dance in Mental Well-being

##### What I discovered through the research

The main finding is that Indian dance is not one uniform system. Classical forms often use codified technique and repertoire, while folk and community dances are closely connected with local celebrations, work, seasons and social participation. Ritual forms may combine music, movement, devotion and collective identity. Even when two traditions share gestures or stories, their rhythm, costume, language, movement quality and performance setting can be different.

#### 1. Music as an Indian Knowledge System

##### Overview

Music in the Indian Knowledge System can be studied as an organised body of knowledge about sound, voice, melody, rhythm, instruments, performance, memory, aesthetics and social life. Sanskrit sources use terms such as śruti (that which is heard), svara (musical note or pitch), tāla (rhythmic cycle), rāga (melodic framework, in later theory), gīta (song), vādya (instrumental music) and nṛtya/nāṭya (dance and dramatic performance). The boundaries between music, poetry, ritual and theatre were often connected rather than separate.

##### History and scope

The earliest surviving textual evidence for highly organised musical recitation is found in the Vedic corpus, especially the Sāmaveda. Later Sanskrit treatises developed technical discussions of notes, intervals, melodic types, rhythm, instruments and performance. Over centuries, music was supported by temples, courts, devotional communities, hereditary performers, teachers, local institutions and, in modern times, universities, academies, radio, recordings and festivals.

##### Ancient textual references

- Sāmaveda: a collection intended for liturgical chanting; its verses are arranged as Sāman chants in specialised song-books.
- Nāṭyaśāstra, traditionally attributed to Bharata: a foundational treatise on theatre and performing arts, including music, notes, instruments, song and rhythm.
- Dattilam: an early independent musicological work on Gandharva music, preserved and studied alongside the Nāṭyaśāstra.
- Bṛhaddeśī, attributed to Mataṅga: an important early text in the history of rāga theory and the distinction between mārga (classical/canonical) and deśī (regional/local) forms.
- Saṅgīta Ratnākara, attributed to Śārṅgadeva (13th century): a major medieval compendium that became influential in later North and South Indian musicological traditions.

##### Cultural meaning

Music has served multiple purposes: sacred recitation, worship, storytelling, entertainment, courtly refinement, community celebration, emotional expression and disciplined artistic training. In many traditions, sound is also discussed philosophically through ideas such as nāda (sound) and the relationship between sound, consciousness and devotion. These philosophical interpretations are important cultural ideas, not laboratory claims.

#### 2. Vedic music and Samagana

##### Overview

Vedic music is most clearly represented by the disciplined recitation and chanting of Vedic texts. The Sāmaveda is especially important because its verses are intended to be sung as Sāmans. Many verses are drawn from the Ṛgveda, but their ritual use and melodic treatment give them a distinct musical role. The Sāmaveda is therefore a major primary source for studying the early relationship between sacred language, pitch and musical performance.

##### History

Vedic recitation developed within oral traditions in which sound, accent, sequence and pronunciation were carefully preserved. The Sāmaveda is associated particularly with the Udgātṛ priest in Soma-sacrifice settings. The verses acquire their musical form through specialised chant traditions and song collections called Gānas. The exact chronology of the Vedic layers is complex; it is safer to describe the Sāmaveda as belonging to the early Vedic textual tradition than to assign a precise modern calendar date to the musical system.

##### Ancient textual references

• Sāmaveda Saṃhitā: the central textual source for Sāman chanting. The Vedic Heritage Portal explains that its verses are chiefly drawn from the eighth and ninth maṇḍalas of the Ṛgveda and are intended for ritual application.\
• Jaiminīya and other Sāmavedic traditions preserve distinct chant lineages and song forms.\
• Śikṣā and Prātiśākhya literature discusses phonetics, articulation, accent and recitation—technical foundations for accurate oral transmission.\
• The Ṛgveda provides hymnic material that is reused and musically arranged in the Sāmaveda; the two should not be treated as identical collections.

##### Rituals and performance contexts

Samagana is connected with Vedic sacrificial performance, especially Soma rituals. Chanting was not simply an aesthetic concert: it was part of a larger liturgical sequence performed by trained priests. The musical delivery, pronunciation and timing were integral to the ritual context.

##### Regional practices

Vedic recitation traditions have been preserved in different parts of India. The Vedic Heritage Portal documents recitation lineages and oral traditions; the portal also records three surviving Sāmaveda recensions (Kauthuma, Jaiminīya and Rāṇāyanīya) and documents the Jaiminīya tradition of Kerala. Regional schools may preserve different śākhās, accents and methods of recitation. UNESCO inscribed the Tradition of Vedic Chanting on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2008.

##### Cultural meaning

The tradition demonstrates how sound can function as a vehicle of memory, knowledge and ritual continuity. Its demanding oral methods preserve linguistic detail across generations. It also shows that Indian musical history includes forms of disciplined chant that are not the same as modern concert-based classical music.

#### 3. Gandharva music and the Natya Shastra

##### Overview

The Nāṭyaśāstra places music within a wider performing-arts framework. Its discussions connect song, instrumental accompaniment, rhythm, dance, drama, emotion and audience experience. The term Gandharva is used in Sanskrit musicological contexts for a structured musical tradition, often discussed in relation to the performance arts and distinguished from Vedic chant.

##### History

The Nāṭyaśāstra took shape over a long period; scholarly reference works give a broad range for its composition and note that its presently known form likely developed around the late centuries BCE and early centuries CE. The text is traditionally attributed to Bharata, but the work is layered and its precise date and authorship remain scholarly questions. Later writers, including commentators and musicologists, continued to interpret and develop its ideas.

##### Ancient textual references

• Nāṭyaśāstra: discusses the seven svaras—Ṣaḍja, Ṛṣabha, Gāndhāra, Madhyama, Pañcama, Dhaivata and Niṣāda—and describes musical structures, instruments and performance.\
• Dattilam: an important early treatise on Gandharva music; IGNCA describes it as complementing and in some respects supplementing the Nāṭyaśāstra.\
• Abhinavabhāratī: Abhinavagupta’s influential commentary on the Nāṭyaśāstra, important for understanding performance and aesthetic theory.\
• Later compendia such as Bṛhaddeśī and Saṅgīta Ratnākara preserve and extend earlier musicological discussions.

##### Rituals and performance contexts

Music in the Nāṭyaśāstra is part of theatre and dance performance. Song, instrumental music and movement are coordinated to support dramatic situations and emotional states. Musical performance may accompany staged narratives and ceremonial or public performance contexts; the text is not simply a handbook for modern classical concerts.

##### Regional practices

The treatise belongs to a pan-Indian Sanskrit intellectual tradition, but later commentarial and performance traditions developed in multiple regions. Its influence is visible in later musicological writing across the subcontinent. The text's technical vocabulary is historically important, though the same terms can change meaning across periods and regions.

##### Cultural meaning

The Nāṭyaśāstra shows that music was theorised as a component of a complex art of communication. It links sound with gesture, dramatic situation, emotional expression and audience response. It is a key source for understanding music as an integrated performing art in classical Indian thought.

#### 4. The development of raga and tala

##### Overview

Rāga and tāla are two central organising ideas in Indian classical music. Rāga is a melodic framework with characteristic notes, movements and phrases; tāla is a recurring rhythmic cycle. They are not merely scales and time signatures: their musical identity depends on rules, emphasis, phrasing, ornamentation and performance practice.

##### History

Early music theory used concepts such as grāma, mūrchanā, śruti, svara and jāti. The Bṛhaddeśī, usually placed somewhere between the 6th and 9th centuries CE (its date is uncertain), is especially significant because IGNCA describes it as the first extant text to describe rāga and to distinguish rāga from the older categories of jāti and mūrchanā. The text survives only in incomplete, reconstructed form. Later texts refined and reorganised these ideas. The modern Hindustani and Carnatic systems developed through long historical processes and should not be projected unchanged into the Vedic period.

##### Ancient textual references

• Nāṭyaśāstra: discusses svaras, grāmas, mūrchanās, jātis and rhythmic organisation.\
• Bṛhaddeśī of Mataṅga: a landmark source for the definition and development of rāga; it also discusses deśī and mārga approaches.\
• Saṅgīta Ratnākara: a major medieval synthesis of music theory, covering vocal music, instruments and dance-related subjects.\
• Later texts such as Caturdaṇḍī Prakāśikā and other regional treatises contribute to the historical formation of later systems.

##### Rituals and performance contexts

Rāga and tāla are performed in devotional, courtly, educational, festival and concert settings. In classical concerts, a rāga may be explored through improvisation and composed material, while tāla organises the rhythmic cycle. Specific rāgas are traditionally associated with times of day, seasons or moods in some lineages; these are aesthetic conventions and performance traditions, not universal rules followed identically by every musician.

##### Regional practices

Rāga and tāla are shared foundations across Hindustani and Carnatic music, but their terminology, repertoire, rhythmic systems and performance conventions differ. Regional and historical schools have developed distinct rāga repertoires and approaches to rhythm. Folk traditions may use melodic and rhythmic organisation of their own without fitting neatly into classical definitions.

##### Cultural meaning

These concepts show a highly developed way of organising musical creativity. A rāga provides identity and constraints while allowing improvisation; tāla provides a cyclical rhythmic framework that supports interaction between performers. Together they balance structure and freedom.

#### 5. Musical instruments and their classification

##### Overview

Indian music uses a wide range of instruments made from wood, metal, skin, bamboo, gourds and strings. Instruments may accompany vocal music, provide melody, maintain rhythm or create ceremonial sound. Bharata's fourfold classification, still widely used in descriptions of Indian instruments, groups them by the way sound is produced.

##### History

Ancient sculptures, temple reliefs, paintings and Sanskrit treatises provide evidence for the long history of instruments. The Nāṭyaśāstra classifies instruments into four broad categories. Modern instruments have changed in construction and playing technique over time; it is therefore important not to assume that an ancient instrument had exactly the same form as its modern namesake.

##### Ancient textual references

• Tat (तत): string instruments, such as vīṇā-type instruments.\
• Avanaddha (अवनद्ध): membranophones, or instruments with a stretched membrane, such as mṛdaṅga-type drums.\
• Suṣira (सुषिर): wind instruments, such as flute and other pipes.\
• Ghana (घन): solid or idiophonic instruments, such as bells and cymbals.\
The Ministry of Culture's Indian Culture portal presents these categories and notes that they are attributed to Bharata's Nāṭyaśāstra.

#### 6. Hindustani classical music

##### Overview

Hindustani music is the major classical tradition associated primarily with northern India. It includes vocal forms such as dhrupad, khayal, thumri and other genres, as well as instrumental traditions. Its central organising concepts include rāga and tāla, with improvisation playing an important role in many performance forms.

##### History

The historical development of Hindustani music involved Sanskrit musicological inheritance, regional court cultures, devotional movements and interaction with Persianate musical and literary environments. The distinction between Hindustani and Carnatic traditions became more pronounced over the medieval and early modern periods; the exact chronology is complex. Mughal and regional courts supported musicians, while later colonial and modern institutions reshaped patronage, teaching and public performance.

##### Ancient textual references

Relevant textual background includes Nāṭyaśāstra, Bṛhaddeśī and Saṅgīta Ratnākara. Later treatises and repertories developed in different regional settings. Persian-language and vernacular sources, court records and oral lineages are also important for studying the tradition's later history. No single ancient text describes the modern Hindustani system in its present form.

##### Rituals and performance contexts

Performance may take place in concerts, temples, courts historically, music festivals, community gatherings and educational settings. A typical classical presentation can include an exposition of the rāga, composed pieces and improvisational development, with the exact structure varying by genre and gharana. Devotional and semi-classical forms may also be associated with particular festivals or social occasions.

##### Regional practices

Important regional centres and lineages include Gwalior, Agra, Jaipur-Atrauli, Kirana, Patiala and other gharanas, alongside dhrupad traditions associated with Dagar and other lineages. These names refer to historically developed stylistic schools, not separate language or state boundaries. North Indian music is diverse and includes regional folk and devotional forms beyond the classical concert tradition.

##### Cultural meaning

Hindustani music demonstrates how a shared theoretical framework can support distinct schools, personal interpretation and improvisation. Gharana traditions preserve specialised knowledge, while concerts and recordings have helped the music reach wider audiences.

#### 7. Carnatic classical music

##### Overview

Carnatic music is the major classical tradition associated primarily with southern India. It is strongly centred on vocal composition and features a rich repertoire of kritis, varnams, padams and other forms, alongside elaborate improvisational practices such as ālāpana, neraval and kalpana svaras. Instruments such as vīṇā, violin, flute, mṛdaṅgam and ghatam are used in performance.

##### History

Carnatic music developed through a long interaction of Sanskrit and regional-language musicology, temple and court patronage, devotional composition and teacher-disciple lineages. Medieval treatises such as Saṅgīta Ratnākara were influential in the broader history of Indian music theory. Later southern texts, including Venkatamakhin's Caturdaṇḍī Prakāśikā, helped shape the theoretical organisation of the modern Carnatic rāga system. The tradition's modern concert repertoire was strongly shaped by composers of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, including the celebrated Carnatic Trinity.

##### Ancient textual references

Ancient and medieval sources provide theoretical foundations, but the modern Carnatic concert system should not be attributed directly to one ancient treatise. Saṅgīta Ratnākara is an important historical bridge; Caturdaṇḍī Prakāśikā is a key later work in discussions of rāga classification. Compositions of Purandara Dāsa and the Trinity are central to the living repertoire, though their works belong to later historical periods.

##### Rituals and performance contexts

Carnatic music is performed in temple settings, devotional gatherings, music sabhas and concert festivals. The annual Chennai December Season is a prominent modern institutional setting, while temple festivals and household devotional practices remain important in many places. The music also accompanies dance forms such as Bharatanatyam.

##### Regional practices

The strongest historical centres include Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Kerala, with important institutions and lineages across the southern region and beyond. The language of compositions includes Telugu, Sanskrit, Tamil, Kannada and other languages. Regional identity is significant, but Carnatic music is performed internationally.

##### Cultural meaning

Carnatic music combines disciplined composition with improvisation and a strong devotional and aesthetic dimension. Its teaching system emphasises repertoire, voice or instrument technique, rhythmic precision and the transmission of stylistic knowledge from teacher to student.

#### 8. Devotional, temple and community music

##### Overview

Devotional music uses singing, rhythm and sometimes dance to express religious devotion, narrate sacred stories and bring communities together. It includes bhajan, kirtan, nāmasankīrtana, abhang, qawwali, temple music, Buddhist chanting and many local forms. These practices are not one single genre; they have different histories, languages, musical structures and religious settings.

##### History

Bhakti movements across different regions encouraged the composition and circulation of devotional poetry and songs in Sanskrit and regional languages. Over time, devotional music developed through temple institutions, wandering poet-saints, community singing, Sufi gatherings and local performance traditions. Specific forms emerged in different historical periods; it would be inaccurate to label all devotional music as ancient Vedic music.

##### Ancient textual references

Texts and repertoires vary by tradition. Devotional songs draw on the Bhagavata Purana, Ramayana traditions, compositions of poet-saints and regional devotional literature. In Manipur, Sankirtana narrates the lives and deeds of Krishna through song and dance. Buddhist chanting in Ladakh involves the recitation of sacred Buddhist texts. These are distinct traditions and should be studied in their own contexts.

##### Rituals and performance contexts

Music may be used during daily worship, temple festivals, life-cycle ceremonies, seasonal festivals, processions and community gatherings. UNESCO documents Sankirtana of Manipur as ritual singing, drumming and dancing associated with religious occasions and stages of life. It also documents Buddhist chanting in Ladakh as part of monastic and village ritual calendars.

##### Regional practices

Examples include kirtan and abhang traditions in Maharashtra; bhajan and temple singing across many regions; Carnatic devotional repertoire in the South; Sankirtana in Manipur; and Buddhist chanting in Ladakh. Sufi qawwali is an important devotional musical tradition in South Asia, with its own historical and institutional contexts.

##### Cultural meaning

Devotional music can create shared identity, preserve poetry, teach religious narratives and strengthen community bonds. It is also a form of embodied knowledge: singers learn texts, melodies, rhythm, gesture and the social rules of performance through participation and training.

#### 9. Folk and regional musical traditions

##### Overview

India's folk and regional music traditions are diverse and closely connected with language, occupation, landscape, community memory, festivals, storytelling, work and local worship. They include seasonal songs, harvest songs, ballads, wedding songs, devotional singing, dance music and instrumental ensembles. Folk music is not simply an earlier stage of classical music; it has its own systems, purposes and aesthetic values.

##### History

Many folk traditions have been transmitted orally over generations, so their beginnings are difficult to date precisely. Historical study often relies on field recordings, ethnography, local histories, performance documentation, instruments and oral testimony. Claims that a particular folk form is thousands of years old should be treated cautiously unless supported by dated evidence.

##### Ancient textual references

Folk repertoires often draw on epics, local legends, devotional poetry and community histories rather than a single pan-Indian musicological text. The Sanskrit distinction between mārga and deśī is useful as a historical idea, but it should not be used to reduce all folk traditions to one category. Modern ethnomusicological research and state cultural archives are important sources.

##### Rituals and performance contexts

Music accompanies harvests, weddings, births, seasonal festivals, rites of passage, religious processions, storytelling and community celebrations. In some traditions, songs are linked to agricultural cycles or specific stages of work; in others, performance is central to ritual or social identity.

##### Regional practices

Examples include Bihu songs and music in Assam; Baul songs of Bengal; Lavani and powada in Maharashtra; Rajasthani Manganiyar and Langa repertoires; Pandavani in Chhattisgarh; Garba and devotional singing in Gujarat; and numerous tribal music traditions across central, eastern, north-eastern and southern India. This list is illustrative and not exhaustive. UNESCO's international inventory includes several Indian musical and performance traditions.

##### Cultural meaning

Folk music preserves local languages, histories, ecological knowledge, social memory and community identity. It also shows that musical knowledge is distributed across communities and is not limited to written treatises or elite court traditions.

#### 10. Guru–shishya parampara, oral transmission and learning

##### Overview

A major feature of many Indian music traditions is oral learning through sustained teacher–student interaction. In classical music this is often described as guru–shishya parampara. Learning includes listening, repetition, memorisation, correction, imitation, practice, repertoire and the gradual development of musical judgement. The process is not identical across all communities or institutions.

##### History and textual context

Oral transmission is especially important in the preservation of Vedic recitation, where elaborate methods of memorisation and recitation help maintain the text and its sound patterns. In later music traditions, hereditary and lineage-based teaching played a major role. Modern music schools, universities, academies, online resources and recorded media now coexist with traditional forms of apprenticeship.

##### Ancient and historical references

Vedic recitation traditions provide a particularly clear example of structured oral preservation. Śikṣā and Prātiśākhya texts discuss sound, pronunciation and recitation. The Sangeet Natak Akademi describes Indian classical music as generally transmitted orally, with students learning over many years from a guru. The details of teaching practices vary among gharanas, bani traditions, regional schools and individual teachers.

##### Cultural meaning

Oral learning preserves nuances that notation alone may not capture: intonation, ornamentation, phrasing, timing, pronunciation, improvisational habits and performance etiquette. It also creates relationships of responsibility between teacher and learner. In contemporary settings, written notation and recordings are useful supports, but they do not fully replace listening-based training.

##### Broad historical timeline (approximate and debated)

- **Vedic period:** Vedic hymns and recitation traditions; Sāmaveda preserves liturgical chants and musical arrangements.
- **Late centuries BCE–early centuries CE (broad scholarly range):** Nāṭyaśāstra takes shape as a major treatise on theatre and performing arts, including music.
- **Date uncertain (generally placed between the Nāṭyaśāstra and the Bṛhaddeśī):** Dattilam, an early independent treatise on Gandharva music.
- **Roughly 6th–9th century CE (debated):** Bṛhaddeśī, attributed to Mataṅga, becomes a landmark in the textual history of rāga.
- **13th century CE:** Saṅgīta Ratnākara by Śārṅgadeva becomes a major medieval synthesis of music theory.
- **Medieval and early modern periods:** Regional, devotional, courtly and temple traditions develop in multiple directions; Hindustani and Carnatic identities become more distinct.
- **18th–19th centuries:** Major composers, lineages and modern repertoires shape both classical traditions; new patronage and performance settings emerge.
- **20th century to present:** Music academies, universities, recordings, radio, festivals and global performance networks reshape teaching and public access.

Timeline caution: these are broad reference points, not exact dates for the beginning of music in India. The development of musical practices is older, more diverse and less linear than a short timeline can show.

### History

Holi began as a spring fire rite linked to the new harvest and later joined the Holika–Prahlada story. The Chutiya copper plate of 1401 CE records a land grant made on Bihu. Govindananda’s 16th-century Varshakriyakaumudi records Saraswati worship on Vasant Panchami in Bengal. Pongal’s roots lie in the Sangam-era Thai Niradal, and al-Biruni described Diwali in north India around 1030 CE. Dates for the Puranas remain approximate and debated.

### Ancient textual references

The Rigveda’s Devi Sukta (10.125) underpins the Shakti theology behind Navratri, and the Devi Mahatmya (c. 5th–6th century CE) is recited during it. Makar Sankranti is tied to Uttarayana in the Bhagavad Gita (8.24) and the Mahabharata, Diwali to Harsha’s Nagananda (7th century) and the Skanda and Padma Puranas, Vasant Panchami to the Taittiriya Aranyaka and Kalidasa’s Ritusamhara, and Holi to the Purva Mimamsa Sutras and Ratnavali.

### Rituals and practice

Holi has a bonfire on the eve and coloured powder the next day. Vasant Panchami has Saraswati Puja and Vidyarambha, when children write their first letters. Sankranti has holy baths and til-gul sweets. Pongal has four days, including boiling rice in a new pot and worshipping cattle. Bihu has Goru Bihu, when cattle are bathed, and dance. Navratri has nine nights of fasting and Garba, and Diwali has lamps, rangoli and five days of observance.

### Cultural meaning

The festivals express gratitude to sun, rain, soil and cattle, renewal through cleaning and burning the old, and the victory of good over evil in the stories of Prahlada, Durga, Rama and Krishna. Holi briefly suspends social hierarchy. UNESCO recognised Durga Puja (2021), Garba (2023) and Deepavali (2025), and the research treats the festivals as a living archive of astronomy, agriculture, ethics and the arts.

##### 2. Holi: The Festival of Colours

Holi is celebrated on the full moon (Purnima) of Phalguna (February–March) and is one of the oldest recorded festivals of India, marking the passage from winter to spring.

###### 2.1 History

- Holi began as a spring fire rite linked to the new harvest, and was later tied to the Holika–Prahlada story of the Puranas ([Curly Tales](https://curlytales.com/surprising-facts-about-holi-youd-have-never-known)).
- An early name was "Holika"; it was originally a rite performed by married women for family well-being, with the full moon (Raka) worshipped. It later became a festival of spring merrymaking, also called Vasanta-Mahotsava and Kama-Mahotsava ([Guyana Chronicle, History of Holi](https://guyanachronicle.com/?p=60211)).
- A word root often cited: roasted, half-ripe grain was called *hola* and offered into the sacred fire before being shared, which is how "Holikotsav" and "Holi" entered common speech.
- A 16th-century panel at a temple in Hampi (Vijayanagara) depicts Holi revelry, and Mughal miniatures of the 17th century show royal Holi celebrations.

###### 2.3 Rituals

1. **Holika Dahan (eve of Holi):** a bonfire is lit at night, symbolising the burning away of evil; people circle it and offer new grain.
2. **Rangwali Holi / Dhulandi (next day):** people smear *gulal* (coloured powder) and spray coloured water, with music and dance.
3. **Regional forms:**
   - Lathmar Holi in Barsana and Nandgaon (Uttar Pradesh)
   - Dol Jatra / Dol Purnima in West Bengal and Odisha
   - Shigmo in Goa
4. **Food:** gujiya, thandai and seasonal sweets shared between households.

###### 2.4 Religious practices

- **Vaishnava:** the Prahlada legend, where Holika, immune to fire, sits with the child Prahlada on a pyre, but she burns and he survives through devotion to Vishnu. It teaches the triumph of bhakti over tyranny.
- **Krishna bhakti:** in Mathura–Vrindavan, Holi celebrates the divine play of Radha and Krishna, the source of the colour-throwing custom.
- **Shaiva:** the legend of Shiva burning Kamadeva, the god of love, is also linked to the festival.
- In Bengal and Odisha, Holi Purnima is the birth anniversary of Sri Chaitanya Mahaprabhu (1486–1533).

- **Social inversion:** for one day, distinctions of caste, age, gender and status are suspended as everyone is coloured alike.
- **Renewal and forgiveness:** old quarrels are set aside; it is a time to restore broken relationships.
- **Seasonal knowledge:** it marks the ripening of the rabi crop and the full arrival of spring.
- **Arts:** Holi inspired Braj folk songs (*hori*, *dhamar*), Rajput and Mughal paintings, and countless film songs.

##### 3. Vasant Panchami: The Festival of Spring and Learning

Vasant Panchami falls on the fifth day of the bright half (Shukla Panchami) of Magha (January–February), marks the onset of spring, and is the principal day of Saraswati worship in India.

###### 3.1 History

- It belongs to a family of ancient spring festivals grouped as *Vasantotsava* ("festival of spring"), alongside Kamotsava/Madanotsava and Holi ([DNA India, citing Nityananda Misra](https://dnaindia.com/india/photo-gallery-9-lesser-known-facts-about-basant-panchami-2718276)).
- In Sanskrit sources the day is called *Shri Panchami*, and historically Lakshmi (Shri), not Saraswati, was worshipped on it ([DNA India](https://www.dnaindia.com/india/photo-gallery-vasant-panchami-2020-9-lesser-known-facts-about-vasantotsava-2810985)).
- The 16th-century Bengali scholar Govindananda, in *Varshakriyakaumudi*, records Saraswati worship and abstention from study on this day as a custom of Gauda (Bengal), showing the regional growth of the Saraswati tradition.
- It is the fourth of six major bathing (*snana*) days at the Magh Mela and Kumbh Mela at Prayag.
- The day also starts the 40-day countdown to Holi.

- **Saraswati:** regarded as the goddess of knowledge, speech, music and the arts, and the day is celebrated as her appearance day.

- **Lakshmi (Shri):** the older textual layer of the festival.

###### 3.5 Cultural meaning

- **Reverence for knowledge:** it places learning, the arts and music at the centre of festive life, a distinctive feature of the Indian tradition.
- **Ecological awareness:** it records the agrarian observation of mustard bloom and the end of winter.
- **Education rites:** Vidyarambha links a seasonal festival to a child's entry into formal education.
- **Literary heritage:** spring (*vasanta*) is the favourite season of Sanskrit poetry, and this festival is its living counterpart.

##### 4. Makar Sankranti: The Sun's Northward Turn

Makar Sankranti marks the Sun's entry into Makara (Capricorn) on 14 or 15 January, and is one of the few Indian festivals fixed to the solar calendar rather than the moon.

###### 4.1 History

- *Makara* means Capricorn and *Sankranti* means transition; the festival celebrates the start of Uttarayana, the Sun's six-month northward course ([The Week](https://www.theweek.in/news/india/2025/01/14/makar-sankranti-significance-of-uttarayan-in-hinduism.html)).
- **An astronomical note:** the winter solstice (true Uttarayana) now falls on about 21 December. Because of the precession of the equinoxes, Makar Sankranti and the solstice drifted apart; they last coincided around 291 CE. The festival thus preserves an ancient astronomical observation.
- It became a harvest festival because it falls when the kharif crop is in and the rabi crop is growing.
- Every twelve years, Makar Sankranti coincides with bathing days of the Kumbh Mela, one of the largest pilgrim gatherings in the world (UNESCO ICH, 2017).

###### 4.2 Ancient textual references

| Text | What it records |
| --- | --- |
| Vedic texts (Brahmanas, Upanishads) | Uttarayana as *devayana*, the "path of the gods", auspicious for ritual and liberation |
| Bhagavad Gita 8.24 | Those who depart in the six months of Uttarayana attain Brahman |
| Mahabharata (Bhishma Parva, Anushasana Parva) | Bhishma, lying on a bed of arrows, waits for Uttarayana before leaving his body |

Sources: [Vajiram & Ravi](https://vajiramandravi.com/current-affairs/makar-sankranti-2025/), [Free Press Journal](https://www.freepressjournal.in/spirituality/guiding-light-makara-sankranti), [Festivals of India](https://www.festivalsofindia.in/makarsankranti/legends).

###### 4.3 Rituals

1. **Holy bath (*snana*)** in the Ganga, Yamuna or other sacred rivers; the Gangasagar Mela in West Bengal draws lakhs of pilgrims.
2. **Surya worship:** offerings of water (*arghya*) to the rising Sun.
3. **Til and jaggery:** sesame-jaggery sweets (tilgul, til laddoo, chikki) are shared; in Maharashtra people say *tilgul ghya, god god bola* ("take sweets, speak sweetly").
4. **Dana (charity):** gifts of sesame, grain, blankets and khichdi to the needy.
5. **Kite flying:** especially in Gujarat (Uttarayan) and Rajasthan.
6. **Bonfires:** Lohri in Punjab on the eve of Sankranti.

| Region | Name |

| Punjab, Haryana | Maghi (Lohri the night before) |
| Tamil Nadu | Pongal |
| Assam | Magh Bihu / Bhogali Bihu |
| Uttar Pradesh, Bihar | Khichdi Parva |
| West Bengal | Poush Sankranti / Poush Parbon |
| Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Karnataka | Sankranti / Sankranthi |
| Uttarakhand | Ghughuti / Uttarayani |
| Odisha | Makara Mela / Makara Chaula |

###### 4.5 Religious practices

- Uttarayana is regarded as the "day" of the gods, so new ventures, rituals and charity are considered especially fruitful.
- **Bhishma legend:** having the boon to choose his time of death, Bhishma waited for Uttarayana to attain liberation.
- **Surya:** the Sun is honoured as the visible source of life, energy and time.

###### 4.6 Cultural meaning

- **Scientific heritage:** the festival is a public record of solar astronomy and calendar-making (*jyotisha*).
- **Unity in diversity:** one astronomical event is celebrated under a dozen names across India.
- **Nutrition knowledge:** sesame and jaggery are warming winter foods in Ayurveda.
- **Social harmony:** sharing sweets with sweet words symbolises ending bitterness.

##### 5. Pongal: The Tamil Harvest Thanksgiving

Pongal (Thai Pongal) is a four-day harvest festival of Tamil Nadu, held from about 14 to 17 January at the start of the Tamil month of Thai, and celebrated by Tamils worldwide ([Britannica](https://britannica.com/topic/Pongal)).

###### 5.1 History

- Its roots are traced to the Sangam Age (c. 300 BCE – 300 CE), when a related observance called *Thai Niradal* ("Thai bathing") was held in the month of Margazhi ([Britannica](https://britannica.com/topic/Pongal)).
- During Thai Niradal, young women observed a vow called *Pavai Nonbu*, fasting and praying for good rain and a rich paddy crop, and ended it on the first day of Thai. Scholars see this as an ancestor of Pongal.
- Sangam texts do not use the word "Pongal" itself; a direct reference to the term appears later, in the 9th-century Tamil epic *Civaka Cintamani* ([Tamil Culture](https://tamilculture.com/the-history-of-pongal-did-you-know-that-there-is-no-reference-to-pongal-in-ancient-tamil-text)).
- An inscription at the Viraraghava Perumal temple, Thiruvallur, also mentions the festival.
- The bull-embracing sport (*eru thazhuvuthal*, today's Jallikattu) is described in Sangam literature about 2,000 years old.

###### 5.2 Ancient textual references

| Text | Period (approx.) | What it records |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sangam anthologies (Paripatal, Narrinai, Kalittokai) | c. 300 BCE – 300 CE | Thai Niradal, the auspiciousness of Thai, bull-taming |
| Andal, *Tiruppavai* | 8th century CE | Margazhi vow (Pavai Nonbu) and dawn bathing |
| Manikkavacakar, *Tiruvempavai* | 9th century CE | Margazhi devotional vow tradition |
| *Civaka Cintamani* | 9th century CE | Direct use of the term "Pongal" |

Sources: [Britannica](https://britannica.com/topic/Pongal), [Tamil Culture](https://tamilculture.com/the-history-of-pongal-did-you-know-that-there-is-no-reference-to-pongal-in-ancient-tamil-text).

###### 5.3 Rituals: the four days

| Day | Name | Main ritual |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 (last day of Margazhi) | Bhogi Pongal | Old belongings are discarded or burnt; homes are cleaned and renewed |
| 2 (1st of Thai) | Surya / Thai Pongal | Rice, milk and jaggery are boiled in a new clay pot in the open until it overflows; offered to the Sun |
| 3 | Mattu Pongal | Cattle are bathed, horns painted, garlanded and worshipped; Jallikattu in some districts |
| 4 | Kaanum Pongal | Family outings, visiting relatives, feeding birds |

- The pot is decorated with turmeric and sugarcane, and *kolam* (rice-flour designs) are drawn at the entrance.
- As the milk boils over, the family shouts "Pongalo Pongal!", an omen of abundance.

###### 5.4 Religious practices

- **Surya:** the main day thanks the Sun god for the harvest; the first portion of pongal is offered to him.
- **Indra** (as giver of rain) is honoured on Bhogi in many households.
- **Cattle:** Mattu Pongal honours the animals that plough the fields and give milk, a recognition of their role in farming.

###### 5.5 Cultural meaning

- The word *pongal* comes from *pongu*, "to boil over", symbolising overflowing prosperity.
- It is called *Tamilar Thirunal*, "the festival of the Tamils", and is central to Tamil identity.
- **Ecological ethic:** gratitude to sun, rain, soil and animals expresses an integrated view of nature found throughout Indian tradition.
- **Social practice:** community cooking and sharing reinforce village solidarity, as field studies of farmers in Tamil Nadu show ([International Research Journal of Tamil, 2022](https://doi.org/10.34256/irjt22220)).

##### 6. Bihu: The Agrarian Festival of Assam

Bihu is the state festival of Assam, celebrated three times a year at key points of the rice-farming calendar, and it blends tribal, Tai-Ahom and Indo-Aryan traditions into one shared Assamese identity.

###### 6.1 The three Bihus

| Bihu | Also called | Time | Agricultural stage | Mood |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rongali Bihu | Bohag Bihu | Mid-April (Baisakh) | Sowing; Assamese New Year | Joy, dance, song |
| Kongali Bihu | Kati Bihu | October–November | Crops growing; granaries empty | Sombre, lamps in fields |
| Bhogali Bihu | Magh Bihu | Mid-January | Harvest complete | Feasting, bonfires |

Sources: [Assam Tourism (official)](https://assamtourism.gov.in/bihu-festival.php), [Sahapedia](https://www.sahapedia.org/bihu-the-festival-of-assam).

###### 6.2 History

- **Indigenous origin:** the word is linked to *Bisu*, "excessive joy", from the Deori and Chutiya languages; early forms were celebrated by the Deori, Chutiya, Sonowal Kachari, Mising, Bodo and other communities.
- **Earliest dated evidence:** a copper-plate inscription of 1401 CE, issued by the Chutiya king Lakshminarayan, records land granted to Brahmins on the auspicious occasion of Bihu ([East India Story](https://eastindiastory.com/bihu-then-now-and-in-between/)).
- **Tai-Ahom contribution:** the Ahoms had their own spring festival (Chyeng-Ken, described in the Khyek-Lai-Bet manuscript) but adopted Bihu; royal patronage, including Bihu performances at the Rang Ghar in Sivasagar, made it a full state festival.
- **Indo-Aryan contribution:** Rongali Bihu was aligned with *Vishuva*, the spring equinox day observed elsewhere in India as Baisakhi, Vishu and Pohela Boishakh.
- **Modern period:** after the Treaty of Yandabo (1826) united Upper and Lower Assam under British rule, Bihu spread across the region. In the 20th century, stage Bihu (*Moncho Bihu*) carried it into public cultural life ([Sentinel Assam](https://sentinelassam.com/editorial/bihu-origin-and-transition-663352)).
- In April 2023, 11,298 performers in Guwahati set the Guinness World Record for the largest Bihu dance ([Guinness World Records](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/99323-largest-bihu-dance)).

###### 6.3 Ancient textual and historical references

| Source | Date | What it records |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chutiya copper-plate of Lakshminarayan (Ghilamara) | 1401 CE | Land grant on the day of Bihu |
| Khyek-Lai-Bet (Ahom manuscript) | Medieval | Rituals of the Ahom spring festival later merged with Bihu |
| Ahom Buranjis (chronicles) | 17th–18th century | Bihu at the Ahom court |
| Shihabuddin Talish's account | 1660s | Mughal chronicler's description of Assamese life during Mir Jumla's campaign |
| Bihu geet and Bonoriya geet (oral) | Pre-literate to present | Folk songs of love, longing and nature |

###### 6.4 Rituals of Rongali Bihu (the seven days, *Xaat Bihu*)

1. **Raati Bihu:** night gatherings with song before the main days.
2. **Goru Bihu:** cattle are bathed in rivers with turmeric and black gram paste, struck lightly with gourd and brinjal, and given new ropes.
3. **Manuh Bihu:** people bathe, wear new clothes and seek elders' blessings; the *gamosa* (handwoven towel) is gifted.
4. **Gosain Bihu:** worship of household deities.
5. **Kutum Bihu:** visiting relatives and sharing pitha and larus.
6. **Mela Bihu:** fairs and public Bihu dances.
7. **Sera Bihu:** farewell to the festival.

- Kati Bihu: earthen lamps (*saki*) are lit at the tulsi plant, the granary and the paddy fields.
- Magh Bihu: bamboo-and-hay huts (*bhelaghar*) and bonfires (*meji*) are built, with community feasts and the *meji* burnt at dawn.

###### 6.5 Religious practices

- Bihu is largely **agrarian and animistic**: it honours cattle, the soil, plants and the fire, rather than a single deity.
- Hindu worship (lamps at the tulsi, prayers to Lord Krishna in Namghars) coexists with tribal rites.
- It is widely regarded as a **secular festival**, celebrated across religion, caste and tribe in Assam.

###### 6.6 Cultural meaning

- **Performing arts:** the Bihu dance and *Bihu geet*, played on dhol, pepa (buffalo-horn pipe), gogona, taal and toka, form a major Indian folk tradition.
- **Fertility and youth:** Rongali Bihu celebrates spring, love and the fertility of land and people.
- **Identity:** Bihu became the central symbol of Assamese cultural nationalism in the 20th century.
- **Key insight:** three festivals mirror three farming stages, showing how agricultural knowledge is coded into the calendar.

##### 7. Navratri: Nine Nights of the Goddess

Navratri ("nine nights") honours the Goddess Durga in her nine forms and ends on the tenth day, Vijayadashami (Dussehra); its main observance, Sharad Navratri, falls in Ashvin (September–October).

###### 7.1 History

- Navratri is observed four times a year at the junctions of the seasons; the two most celebrated are **Chaitra (Vasanta) Navratri** in spring and **Sharad Navratri** in autumn ([Drik Panchang](https://www.drikpanchang.com/navratri/info/navratri.html)).
- Its central myth is the Goddess's battle against the buffalo-demon Mahishasura and her victory on the tenth day.
- In eastern India the same period is celebrated as **Durga Puja**; the public, community (*sarbojanin*) form developed in Bengal from the 18th–19th centuries.
- In Gujarat, Navratri is the setting for **Garba**, a circular devotional dance around a lamp-lit pot; Garba in its present form dates largely from the early 20th century, though village traditions are much older.

###### 7.2 Ancient textual references

| Text | Period (approx.) | What it records |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rigveda, Devi Sukta (10.125) | Vedic | The Goddess (Vak) declaring herself the power behind all gods; root of Shakti theology |
| Devi Mahatmya (Durga Saptashati), in the Markandeya Purana | c. 5th–6th century CE | 700 verses on Durga's victory over Mahishasura, Madhu-Kaitabha, Shumbha-Nishumbha; recited during Navratri |
| Devi Bhagavata Purana | c. 9th–14th century CE | Rules for Navratri worship and Rama's worship of the Goddess before battle |
| Kalika Purana | c. 10th century CE | Autumnal Durga worship (the basis of Bengal's Durga Puja) |
| Valmiki Ramayana / Krittivasi Ramayana | Ancient / 15th century | Rama's victory over Ravana (Vijayadashami); *akal bodhan*, Rama's untimely invocation of Durga, in the Bengali version |

Sources: [Wikipedia: Navaratri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navaratri), [Incredible India: Navratri](https://www.incredibleindia.gov.in/en/festivals-and-events/navratri).

###### 7.3 The nine forms (Navadurga)

| Day | Form | Aspect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Shailaputri | Daughter of the mountain |
| 2 | Brahmacharini | Austerity and devotion |
| 3 | Chandraghanta | Courage, the bell-shaped moon |
| 4 | Kushmanda | Creator of the cosmic egg |
| 5 | Skandamata | Mother of Skanda (Kartikeya) |
| 6 | Katyayani | The warrior goddess |
| 7 | Kalaratri | Destroyer of darkness |
| 8 | Mahagauri | Purity and calm |
| 9 | Siddhidatri | Giver of spiritual powers |

###### 7.4 Rituals

1. **Ghatasthapana:** a sacred pot is installed on day 1, with barley sown beside it to sprout over nine days.
2. **Fasting (*vrata*):** many devotees eat one meal a day or only fruit and specific grains.
3. **Recitation** of the Durga Saptashati and devotional songs.
4. **Kanya Puja:** young girls are worshipped as forms of the Goddess on day 8 or 9.
5. **Ayudha Puja** (South India): tools, books, vehicles and instruments are worshipped on Mahanavami; *Vidyarambham* (first learning) follows on Vijayadashami.
6. **Regional forms:** Garba and Dandiya Raas (Gujarat), Durga Puja pandals (West Bengal), Golu doll displays (Tamil Nadu, Karnataka), Bathukamma flower festival (Telangana), Mysuru Dasara (Karnataka), Kullu Dussehra (Himachal Pradesh) and Ramlila with Ravana effigies (North India).

###### 7.5 Religious practices

- **Shakta:** the Goddess is worshipped as Shakti, the supreme cosmic energy; the nine nights are often grouped in threes for Durga, Lakshmi and Saraswati.
- **Vaishnava:** in North India, Navratri culminates in Rama's victory over Ravana (Dussehra).
- **Philosophical meaning:** the battle against Mahishasura is read as the victory of discernment over ignorance and ego.

###### 7.6 Cultural meaning and recognition

- **UNESCO:** "Durga Puja in Kolkata" was inscribed on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2021 ([UNESCO ICH](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/00703)), and "Garba of Gujarat" in 2023 ([UNESCO ICH](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/garba-of-gujarat-01962)).
- **Social equality:** UNESCO noted that Garba dilutes socio-economic, gender and sect boundaries through shared participation.
- **Arts and crafts:** idol-making (Kumartuli, Kolkata), pandal design, textiles, music and dance sustain large artisan economies.
- **Reverence for the feminine:** the festival celebrates woman as power, mother and wisdom.

##### 8. Diwali (Deepavali): The Festival of Lights

Diwali is a five-day festival centred on the new moon (Amavasya) of Kartika (October–November), and on 10 December 2025 it was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, India's 16th element on that list ([News On AIR](https://www.newsonair.gov.in/unesco-adds-deepavali-to-intangible-cultural-heritage-list), [PIB](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2025/dec/doc20251210725901.pdf)).

###### 8.1 History

- *Deepavali* means "row of lamps" (*deepa* + *avali*). It likely grew from ancient post-harvest festivals of the Kartika month.
- The scholar P. K. Gode traced its history through written records, noting references in the Nilamata Purana of Kashmir and Harsha's *Nagananda* in the 6th–7th centuries CE ([JSTOR Daily](https://daily.jstor.org/a-history-of-diwali)).
- The Persian scholar al-Biruni described Diwali in north India around 1030 CE, and records show it in the Deccan (Kolhapur) in the 11th century.
- Abul Fazl's *Ain-i-Akbari* (1590) describes Diwali at Akbar's court; colonial scholars such as Sir William Jones (1799) also documented its five days.

###### 8.2 Ancient textual references

| Text | Period (approx.) | What it records |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Katha Upanishad | 1st millennium BCE | Story of Nachiketa and Yama, linked in some regions to Diwali night |
| Valmiki Ramayana | Ancient | Rama's return to Ayodhya after defeating Ravana |
| Skanda Purana (Vaishnava Khanda, Kartika Masa Mahatmya) | 1st millennium CE | Rituals for Dhanteras, Naraka Chaturdashi, Diwali; lamps as parts of the Sun; worship of King Bali |
| Padma Purana (chapter on Dipavali) | 1st millennium CE | Importance of lighting lamps; the day after Diwali as New Year |
| Nilamata Purana (Kashmir) | c. 6th–8th century CE | Lamp festival in Kartika |
| Harsha, *Nagananda* | 7th century CE | *Deepapratipadotsava*: lamps lit and gifts given to newly engaged couples |
| Rajashekhara, *Kavyamimamsa* | 9th century CE | *Dipamalika*: homes whitewashed and lit with lamps |
| Jinasena, Harivamsa Purana (Jain) | 8th century CE | Diwali as the nirvana day of Mahavira |

Sources: [JSTOR Daily](https://daily.jstor.org/a-history-of-diwali), [Desi Talk e-paper](https://epaper.desitalk.com/2025_10_24/12/), [The Daily Star](https://archive.thedailystar.net/2005/11/02/d511021501114.htm).

###### 8.3 Rituals: the five days

| Day | Name | Main ritual |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Dhanteras (Dhanatrayodashi) | Buying metal utensils or gold; worship of Dhanvantari, god of Ayurveda; lamp for Yama |
| 2 | Naraka Chaturdashi (Choti Diwali) | Early oil bath; celebrates Krishna's victory over Narakasura |
| 3 | Lakshmi Puja (Amavasya) | Homes lit with diyas; worship of Lakshmi and Ganesha; Kali Puja in Bengal |
| 4 | Govardhan Puja / Bali Pratipada | Annakut (mountain of food) offered to Krishna; King Bali honoured; New Year in Gujarat |
| 5 | Bhai Dooj (Yama Dwitiya) | Sisters pray for brothers' well-being |

- Houses are cleaned and whitewashed; rangoli is drawn at doorways; sweets and gifts are exchanged.
- Traders open new account books (*chopda pujan*), marking the new financial year.

###### 8.4 Religious practices across traditions

| Tradition | What Diwali commemorates |
| --- | --- |
| Hindu (Vaishnava) | Return of Rama, Sita and Lakshmana to Ayodhya; Krishna's defeat of Narakasura; Lakshmi's emergence from the churning of the ocean |
| Hindu (Shakta, Bengal and Assam) | Worship of Goddess Kali |
| Jain | Nirvana of Mahavira, the 24th Tirthankara |
| Sikh | Bandi Chhor Divas: release of Guru Hargobind from Gwalior fort |
| Newar Buddhist (Nepal) | Lakshmi worship during Tihar/Swanti |

###### 8.5 Cultural meaning

- **Philosophical core:** light over darkness, knowledge over ignorance; the Upanishadic prayer *tamaso ma jyotirgamaya* ("lead me from darkness to light") is its motto.
- **Economic life:** the festival renews trade, crafts and the potters' and artisans' economies.
- **Family and community:** reunion, gift-giving and sharing food.
- **Global heritage:** UNESCO recognised it as a living, community-based tradition passed down across generations.

##### 9. Comparative Analysis and Conclusion

The seven festivals share one structure: an astronomical moment, an agricultural stage, a sacred narrative and a community practice, which together make each festival a complete cultural tradition.

###### 9.1 Comparison across the five study heads

| Festival | Earliest textual / dated evidence | Key ritual | Deity or focus | Knowledge domain highlighted |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Makar Sankranti | Vedic Uttarayana; Mahabharata | Holy bath, til-gud, charity | Surya | Astronomy, calendar |
| Pongal | Sangam Thai Niradal; Civaka Cintamani (9th c.) | Boiling the pongal pot | Surya, cattle | Agriculture, ecology |
| Vasant Panchami | Taittiriya Aranyaka; Ritusamhara | Saraswati Puja, Vidyarambha | Saraswati (earlier Shri) | Education, arts |
| Holi | Purva Mimamsa Sutras; Ratnavali (7th c.) | Holika Dahan, colours | Vishnu/Prahlada, Krishna | Social ethics, seasons |
| Bihu | Chutiya copper-plate (1401 CE) | Goru Bihu, Bihu dance | Nature, cattle, fire | Farming cycle, folk arts |
| Navratri | Devi Mahatmya (c. 5th–6th c.) | Ghatasthapana, Garba, Durga Puja | Durga / Shakti | Philosophy, performing arts |
| Diwali | Puranas; Nagananda (7th c.) | Lamps, Lakshmi Puja | Lakshmi, Rama, Krishna | Ethics, economy |

###### 9.2 Common themes

- **Time-keeping:** both solar festivals (Sankranti, Pongal, Bihu) and lunar festivals (Holi, Vasant Panchami, Navratri, Diwali) show the dual luni-solar calendar of India.
- **Gratitude to nature:** sun, rain, soil, rivers, cattle and crops are honoured as partners in life.
- **Victory of good over evil:** Prahlada over Hiranyakashipu, Durga over Mahishasura, Rama over Ravana, Krishna over Narakasura.
- **Renewal:** cleaning homes, burning the old (Bhogi, Holika, Meji), wearing new clothes, starting new account books.
- **Inclusiveness:** Holi's social inversion, Bihu's secular character and Garba's equality are recognised by scholars and UNESCO alike.

###### 9.3 Conclusion

These festivals are not only religious occasions; they are a living archive of India's astronomy, agriculture, ethics, literature and arts. Their continuity from Vedic, Sangam and Puranic sources to present-day public celebration shows how knowledge and values pass from generation to generation through practice, not texts alone. Together they reflect an integrated relationship between the cosmos, nature and society.

## Drawing and painting

This research traces drawing and painting from prehistoric rock shelters at Bhimbetka (c. 8000–3000 BCE) to the Ajanta and Bagh caves. Line, or rekha, is presented as the foundation of Indian visual culture, and the paper checks site evidence against canonical treatises.

### History

Mesolithic artists at Bhimbetka, in Madhya Pradesh, drew hunting scenes, communal dances and gaur in single-stroke contours. By the Gupta and Vakataka periods (4th–6th century CE), painting had been codified into six limbs, the Shadanga, recorded in Yashodhara’s 10th-century commentary Jayamangala on the Kamasutra. Ajanta’s murals in Maharashtra belong to two phases: the Satavahana (c. 2nd century BCE–1st century CE) and the Vakataka under Harishena (c. 5th–6th century CE).

### Ancient textual references

The Chitrasutra of the Vishnudharmottara Purana (Part 3, chapters 35–43) is the main painting manual, covering surface preparation, proportion (talamana) and pigments. The Samarangana Sutradhara (11th century) and Aparajitapriccha (12th century) add instruments, brushes and iconographic grids.

### Technique and regional practice

Ajanta’s murals use fresco-secco, painted on dried plaster over a base of mud, clay, dung and rice husks, with red-ochre outlines under tempera. The palette includes red ochre, yellow ochre, green from malachite, lapis lazuli blue traded from Badakhshan, conch or lime white and lampblack. Bagh shows bolder procession scenes, Ellora’s Kailasa temple (8th–9th century) sharp angular figures, and Sittanavasal (7th–9th century) Jain lotus-pond scenes.

### Cultural meaning

The paper argues that drawing recorded narrative, supported spiritual veneration and ritual, and carried rasa, or emotional resonance. Ajanta’s Buddhist murals show Jataka tales and scenes from the Buddha’s life, and line remained central to the tradition from Bhimbetka to Ajanta.

##### ABSTRACT

This research paper examines the unbroken lineage of visual drawing and painting traditions in ancient India. Spanning from Upper Paleolithic and Mesolithic shelter drawings of Bhimbetka (c.

#### 1. INTRODUCTION & HISTORICAL CONTEXT

Drawing in ancient India was not merely ornamental; it served as a primary cognitive tool for narrative recording, spiritual veneration, and community ritual. The earliest inhabitants of the Indian subcontinent utilized naturally occurring mineral oxides to map their environment, floral and faunal biodiversity, and early societal rituals onto natural rock surfaces (Wakankar, 1976).

As Indian urban civilization matured through the Vedic and Classical periods, these spontaneous rock art traditions evolved into systematic visual canons. Drawing (\*Rekha\*) became categorized as the principal element among the visual arts (\*Chitra\*), preceding color application (\*Vartana\*) and shading. Under royal and monastic patronage, ancient artists codified rules of proportion, posture, gesture (\*Mudra\*), and emotional mood (\*Rasa\*), laying the groundwork for India's world-renowned mural heritage (Sivaramamurti, 1978).

#### 4. CLASSICAL WALL PAINTINGS: THE AJANTA CAVES

The Ajanta Caves in Maharashtra represent the high-water mark of classical Indian mural art. Executed in two major phases—the Satavahana phase (c. 2nd BCE–1st CE) and the Vakataka phase under King Harishena (c. 5th–6th century CE)—these rock-cut Buddhist monasteries contain extensive painted narratives depicting the \*Jataka\* tales and events from the Buddha's life (Spink, 2005).

#### 4.1 Technical Execution (\*Fresco-Secco\*)

Ajanta's murals were not painted on wet plaster (\*Fresco Buono\*), but on dried ground (\*Fresco-Secco\*). The rock surface was first roughened, then covered with a base layer (1–2 cm thick) composed of mud, clay, ferruginous earth, organic cow dung, rice husks, and vegetal binding fibers. Over this, a fine top layer of slaked lime plaster (\*Vajralepa\*) was smoothed and burnished. The master artist (\*Sutradhara\*) drew the preliminary outlines in red ochre (\*Dhāturāga\*) before applying tempera pigments (Ghosh, 1967).

#### 8. CONCLUSION

The evolution of drawing in ancient India reveals a seamless transition from spontaneous prehistoric rock art to a highly sophisticated, textually codified tradition of classical painting. Ancient Indian master artists treated drawing not merely as an initial sketch layer, but as the essential vehicle for spiritual expression, emotional resonance (\*Rasa\*), and enduring artistic identity.
